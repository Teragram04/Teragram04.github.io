Background¶

In 2016, Justin Grimmer published the influential study Appropriators Not Position Takers: The Distorting Effects of Electoral Incentives on Congressional Representation.
This work analyzed press releases from 2006 to 2008, examining how senators’ public statements reflected the partisan composition of their districts.

The Rise of Twitter in Congressional Communication¶

  • In 2006, the microblogging platform then known as twttr debuted in the App Store.
  • By 2009, researchers at the University of Maryland identified 69 members of Congress actively using the platform.
  • According to Golbeck et al. (2010), Twitter was initially used mostly for information sharing, not meaningful political signaling.

With no established norms for engagement, congressional use of Twitter in its early phase was often impersonal, restrained, and non-partisan.

However, by 2016, the landscape had shifted dramatically:

  • Twitter and other platforms were actively shaping political communication strategies (Kreiss & McGregor, 2018).
  • With 71% of American adults getting their news from social media (Pew Research, 2023), digital platforms had become central to senators’ homestyle strategies.

Revisiting Grimmer's Theory: Electoral Incentives and District Alignment¶

Grimmer classifies congressional districts into two categories:

  • Marginal Districts: Partisan balance is relatively even.
  • Aligned Districts: One party significantly dominates the electorate.

Grimmer’s Core Hypotheses:¶

  • Marginal senators avoid ideological positions to prevent alienating opposition voters.
    → Instead, they emphasize appropriations and non-controversial messaging.
  • Aligned senators, secure in their party’s dominance, are incentivized to:
    • Defend party platforms strongly.
    • Take clear ideological positions.
    • Adopt less moderate stances overall.

As a result, Grimmer concludes that congressional discourse appears more polarized than it actually is—driven not by ideology, but by strategic communication incentives.

Our Central Research Question¶

We ask:

Do Grimmer’s findings on press releases still hold in the age of social media—especially on Twitter?

Given the informal, high-frequency nature of digital platforms, we explore whether Twitter has amplified, muted, or reshaped the electoral incentives described by Grimmer.

Design¶

Key Questions¶

To answer whether Grimmer's findings hold equally among twitter posts. We also wanted to examine the possibility that other characteristics (income, diversity) may be predictors of a Senator's homestyle. The following questions, in aggregate help us assess whether this is true.

Neccessary Questions

  1. Is frequency of bias significantly correlated with party margin in posts?

  2. Is message content significantly correlated with party margin in posts?

  3. Is frequency of bias in posts significantly correlated with income?

  4. Is message content in posts significantly correlated with income?

  5. Is frequency of bias in posts significantly correlated diversity?

  6. Is message content in posts significantly correlated diversity?

The Datasets¶

  1. "Political Social Media Posts", pulled from Kaggle. This dataset provides a sample of 5,000 social media posts from various politicians, and categorizes them based on a myriad of variables such as bias, intended audience, a unique id for the politician, and more. This dataset will allow us to determine which kind of message politicians use the most to garner support online- e.g., do politicans favor a national audience, or one primarily composed of their constituency?
  2. 2015 Census Data, Kaggle. Includes population of each group (Black, White, Hispanic, Native American, Asian, Other), as well as income per capita figures organized by county
  3. Historical & Current Legislators, list the party affiliations of all legislators from https://unitedstates.github.io/congress-legislators/
  4. County Census and Election Results, we created using data from https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/statistics/elections/2012 and https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/statistics/elections/2016
  5. A few other csv's can be found in our repository, which helped us fill in the party affiliations of senators who may have had multiple names (ex. Joe/ Joseph). We gathered these using data scraping and Wikipedia and turned them into a csv to save time and memory in this notebook.

Reference: Precise Message Type Definitions¶

  • attack: the message attacks another politician.

  • constituency: the message discusses the politician's constituency.

  • information: an informational message about news in government or the wider U.S.

  • media: a message about interaction with the media.

  • mobilization: a message intended to mobilize supporters.

  • other: a catch-all category for messages that don't fit into the others.

  • personal: a personal message, usually expressing sympathy, support or condolences, or other personal opinions.

  • policy: information about political policy.

  • support: a message of political support.

Hypothesis¶

We predict that if Grimmer’s findings hold, we would find a strong positive correlation between mobilization, policy, personal messages and aligned districts. We further hypothesize a strong positive correlation between constituency, policy messages and marginal districts. We hypothesize, in line with Grimmers assertions, neither diversity nor income will be significant predictors of homestyle.

Variables¶

  • Independent: Senator margins, Diversity, Income
  • Dependent: Message bias, Message frequency

Testing our Hypotheses¶

Party analysis: There were 3 independents in the Senate in 2015, Joe Manchin, Angus King, Bernie Sanders. All have or still do caucus with dems but should still be excluded from trends in party behavior

Given the large shifts in presidential election from 2012 to 2016, 4 metrics are neccessary for a robust understanding of the senators' home partisan makeup

from scipy.stats import linregress
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

# Define message types
message_types = ['attack', 'constituency', 'information', 'media',
                 'mobilization', 'other', 'personal', 'policy', 'support']

# Calculate total number of messages per row
message_frequency['total_messages'] = message_frequency[message_types].sum(axis=1)

# Normalize message types to proportions
for msg in message_types:
    message_frequency[f'{msg}_prop'] = message_frequency[msg] / message_frequency['total_messages']

# Scale Income and avg
scaler = StandardScaler()
message_frequency[['Income', 'avg']] = scaler.fit_transform(message_frequency[['Income', 'avg']])

# Now, regress using proportions
results = []

for msg in message_types:
    x = message_frequency['avg']
    y = message_frequency[f'{msg}_prop']  # use the normalized proportions

    # Drop any rows with missing values (if any)
    mask = (~x.isna()) & (~y.isna())
    x = x[mask]
    y = y[mask]

    slope, intercept, r_value, p_value, std_err = linregress(x, y)

    results.append({
        'Message Type': msg,
        'Slope': slope,
        'Intercept': intercept,
        'p-value': p_value,
        'R-squared': r_value**2
    })

Findings and implications¶

Interpretation and Implications¶

Although differences in how message content is categorized limit direct comparison with Grimmer’s work, several implications still emerge from our findings.

Information and Policy Messaging Is Uniform Across Districts¶

  • Both information and policy tweets were shared at similar rates across aligned and marginal districts.
  • Importantly, messages expressing support for specific policies were categorized separately from neutral policy content.

This suggests a baseline use of Twitter for informational parity, likely stemming from early norms of digital political communication—where tweets were primarily used to share updates and avoid controversy.

Platform Format May Influence Tone¶

Twitter’s structure—informal, high-frequency, and low-cost—could contribute to:

  • Aligned legislators feeling less pressure to use Twitter strictly for ideological promotion.
  • Universal use of the platform for appropriations and policy updates, mimicking press releases.

Unexpected Trend: More Constituency Messages in Aligned Districts¶

  • We found a higher frequency of constituency-oriented messages from aligned legislators.
  • This contradicts Grimmer’s model, where marginal legislators are expected to emphasize constituent services to avoid ideological conflict.

A possible explanation: aligned legislators may feel comfortable generalizing about shared district values, whereas marginal legislators avoid doing so due to ideological diversity.

Personal Branding as a New Marginal Strategy¶

The increased use of personal messages by marginal legislators suggests a strategic shift:

  • Rather than taking overt policy stances, they promote personal values, relatability, and lifestyle content.
  • Examples include posts featuring family, religious activities, or community events—messages that would be unusual in formal press releases but are natural on social media.

This behavior reflects an adaptive form of homestyle that allows marginal senators to signal bipartisan appeal without polarizing policy declarations.

Temporal & Cultural Limitation¶

Our research analyzes congressional behavior in 2015, whereas Grimmer’s conclusions are based on 2006–2008 data. The gap includes:

  • A significant rise in social media usage.
  • Shifts in voter ideology and partisan dynamics.

As a result, we cannot conclusively attribute the observed changes to Twitter alone. Congress itself may have evolved.

New Voter Alignments as a Mechanism¶

A possible explanation for changes in strategy is the decline of true moderates and rise of independents with strong, non-aligned views.

  • Rather than seeing parties as equally favorable, these voters hold intense but ideologically mixed opinions (Broockman & Kalla, 2023).
  • Marginal representatives may respond by taking strong positions across party lines—a strategy suited for appealing to independents.

For example, Joe Manchin posted 66% more policy tweets than the average senator, suggesting strategic engagement even in marginal districts.

Future Research Needed¶

To fully isolate whether these patterns stem from Twitter's affordances or structural shifts in Congress, we recommend:

  • A comparative analysis of press releases and tweets post-2020.
  • Inclusion of constituency demographics (e.g. income, diversity) as control variables.

Final Reflection¶

Regardless of the source, one reality holds:

On Twitter, every senator appears to be a position-taker.

This challenges Grimmer’s original conclusion and raises a pressing question:
Is Congress truly more polarized now—or are digital platforms amplifying partisan cues beyond reality?

Reconsidering Grimmer: Income and Diversity as Potential Confounders¶

Our research revealed statistically significant correlations between constituency income and diversity and the frequency of specific message types shared on Twitter. These findings suggest that socioeconomic and demographic factors—not accounted for in Grimmer’s 2006–2008 press release study—could confound or qualify his conclusions.

Our Supporting Findings¶

  • Information messages were slightly negatively correlated with median income.
  • Mobilization messages were slightly negatively correlated with both income and diversity.
  • Personal messages were moderately positively correlated with diversity.

These correlations suggest that lower-income and more diverse constituencies receive more frequent informational and personalized content, potentially reflecting efforts by senators to build trust or expand cross-cutting appeal, especially in heterogeneous districts.

2008–2015 Demographic Shifts and Political Margins¶

Significant demographic shifts occurred between 2008 and 2015 that likely altered the partisan landscape and the representational incentives Grimmer modeled:

  • Increasing racial and ethnic diversity: According to Pew (2015), the U.S. saw a 2.5% increase in non-white voters between 2008 and 2015, with Latinos and Asian Americans driving much of this growth. These groups tend to live in urban, often Democratic-leaning districts, reducing ideological homogeneity even in "aligned" areas.

  • Urbanization and education growth: More voters moved to urban centers, and college completion rose. Urban areas tend to be ideologically left-leaning but can be internally diverse in priorities and identity politics, weakening the concept of a uniformly aligned district.

  • Widening income inequality: Economic stress during and after the Great Recession increased class-based distinctions in political messaging. Senators may have shifted strategies to address economic grievances and mobilize disaffected voters online.

These shifts indicate that district alignment (as used in Grimmer’s model) may now mask important variations within districts—particularly among marginal or transitioning areas. What once seemed like a partisan alignment might actually involve intraparty diversity or emerging independent ideologies.

Implication: Expanded Model for Digital Homestyle¶

Given Twitter’s informality and frequency, it allows for new forms of homestyle representation:

  • Personal branding (as seen in higher personal tweets in diverse districts)
  • Equity-based communication strategies
  • Digital outreach to underrepresented or politically disengaged groups

This evidence suggests that income and diversity should be modeled as independent variables in future studies evaluating message tone, content, and frequency—especially on digital platforms.

Grimmer’s model provides a strong foundation, but our work suggests it should be revisited with a multi-dimensional view of constituency characteristics that includes race, income, and education alongside partisan composition.
